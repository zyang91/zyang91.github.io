# Ban Philosophy: What Should Policy Do When We Do Not Know?

On September 2, 2026, New York City announced a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI for students from 2-K through eighth grade. Nearly 600,000 students are affected. High school students will face a different system: limited use of approved AI programs, several closely supervised pilots, and required AI literacy modules. The city also plans to spend the coming school year reviewing the policy before determining what comes next. ([Source](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/policies/guidance-on-artificial-intelligence))

Reading this policy brought me back to a question that has circled in my mind for a long time:

**When something, like advanced technology, may both benefit and harm children, should our default response be to ban it?**

My instinctive answer has long been no. But that answer did not begin with AI, nor did it begin with any policy theory I learned in graduate school. I think it began much earlier, with the way I was raised.

## My Family's Teaching Philosophy

If I were asked to use one word to describe my family's philosophy of education, I would choose **“explore.”**

Growing up in China, I was fortunate that my mother understood education from both inside the classroom and inside the education system. She began her career as a public primary school mathematics teacher. After more than fifteen years of teaching, she moved into educational research at our local district education department, focusing on teacher development and evaluation.

Yet I was never an extraordinary student by many conventional definitions in China. I was not the student who consistently ranked at the top of every exam, nor was I particularly accomplished in extracurricular activities. My teachers were more likely to describe me as a quiet, hardworking student than as one of the “elite” students at the top of the class.

China is well known for its exam-centered system of education and student selection. Test scores and rankings can shape which schools students enter and, in turn, the opportunities available to them later. Students with higher rankings may enter the most competitive high schools and follow a relatively clear path toward prestigious universities after the extreme rigrious college entry exam, known as "Gao Kao".

I followed a much more ordinary path. I entered a middle-ranked local high school. My mother largely attributed this to my own learning habits. I was not particularly willing to spend time memorizing materials simply to reproduce them on an exam, nor did I develop the habit of meticulously checking every answer after finishing a test. For various reasons, I frequently made careless mistakes. As a result, my test scores did not always reflect what I actually knew or what I could have answered correctly.

Looking back at middle school and high school, especially when I began preparing for the high school entrance examination, commonly known as the *Zhong Kao*, I remember having little interest in many of the subjects that were mandatory for the exam. I could pass them without much difficulty and usually ranked around 12th or 13th in a class of 45 students. But I rarely had the motivation to memorize what were called the “standard answers” because the examination authority had decided that those were the correct answers.

One example from Chinese class has stayed in my mind ever since: reading comprehension.

Students would be given a passage followed by several short-answer questions. One type of question appeared again and again: *shangxi* (赏析), which roughly asks students to analyze and appreciate a particular sentence or rhetorical expression.

I always found this kind of question strangely arbitrary. Different students could read the same sentence and come away with different interpretations. They might notice different details, make different connections, or simply disagree about whether a particular rhetorical choice was effective.

Yet the examination needed a standardized answer.

There was almost a template for it: *This rhetorical device vividly expresses XXX, which helps demonstrate XXX.*

The second “XXX” is super important. It was not enough to explain what **you** saw in the passage. You needed to identify the particular point that the test designer expected you to see. If your interpretation did not hit that point, even if you could explain it reasonably, you could lose the points.

My problem was not that I could not learn this template. I could. I had very little motivation to memorize an interpretation because someone had already decided that it was the correct one.

Geography was an even more striking example.

I have had a deep passion for geography for as long as I can remember. By the time I was seven, I had already developed a strong sense of direction and learned how to navigate with maps. I could sometimes make sense of complicated maps that even my father found difficult to navigate. When I was twelve or thirteen, before online navigation became something I routinely used, I could design routes for family road trips using printed road atlases. By fifteen, even in places I had never visited before, I could often identify north and orient myself using maps, landmarks, and relative spatial references. My family was frequently surprised by this ability because navigation that seemed intuitive to me did not always come as naturally to them.

Much later, geography became my undergraduate major at Binghamton University. I excelled in the subject, earning an almost perfect major GPA and graduating as one of the strongest geography students in my cohort.

Yet there was a strange period in middle and high school when, despite all of this interest, I could barely navigate the geography exam.

The exams often seemed designed to test obscure pieces of knowledge or catch students with carefully constructed details. Short-answer questions came with predetermined scoring points that did not always seem fully justified to me. I found this particularly frustrating when questions involved spatial reasoning from maps. I might be able to read the map and develop my own reasoning about the spatial relationship being shown, but that did not necessarily mean I would arrive at the particular answer—or the particular reasoning process—the test expected.

Geography, a subject that felt intuitive, analytical, and exploratory to me outside the classroom, increasingly became an exercise in memorizing what I was expected to reproduce inside the classroom.

The way geography was taught in China did not always help. I often felt that some concepts were presented as conclusions to remember rather than questions to understand. When I could not understand why a geographic pattern occurred, the explanation sometimes amounted to another fact, rule, or relationship that I was expected to memorize. That might have helped me answer a particular examination question, but it did not necessarily resolve the question in my mind.

This was especially frustrating because geography, at least as I understood it, was fundamentally about relationships. Why does this city develop here rather than there? Why does a climate pattern change across space? Why does a transportation route follow one corridor instead of another? A map was interesting to me precisely because I could use the information on it to reason about something I had never seen before.

But that was not always how I experienced geography in school. Sometimes, I felt that I was expected to start with the answer and learn how the map was supposed to justify it.

At one point, when I finally had the opportunity to choose which subjects I wanted to continue studying, I even considered giving up geography.

I wanted the freedom to explore the things that genuinely interested me. I want to keep digging into the fields I was passionate about rather than follow a path that had already been designed for me. I wanted to learn how to decide what I should pursue, rather than always being told what I was supposed to pursue.

My family was open to this.

Their confidence did not come from believing that every choice I made would be correct. Instead, they trusted that years of family education and habit-building had given me enough tools to navigate my own path: **to explore, make choices, encounter mistakes, and adjust when necessary.**

**“Explore”** did not mean growing up without boundaries. It meant having enough preparation to make choices without having every choice made for me.

One opportunity to explore came when my high school organized a short exchange program with Miami University in Ohio through the school’s study-abroad program.

My family encouraged me to go. They paid the program expenses and, more importantly, gave me the freedom to treat the trip as an opportunity to see something beyond the educational environment I already knew.

The program was short—only about twenty days. But for the first time, I was immersed in a different culture and a world that felt much larger than the path I had previously imagined for myself. It's not enough for me to understand American education, nor was it enough to know what studying abroad would actually be like.

But it was enough for me to want to explore further.

When I returned home, I made a big decision that changed my life.

## My big decision: Study Abroad

When I returned home, I announced that I wanted to study abroad for my bachelor’s degree.

My family did not immediately tell me yes. They reminded me that studying abroad would come with difficulties, including many that neither they nor I could foresee at the time. I would be leaving the educational system I knew, moving to another country at a young age, studying in a different language, and navigating a culture that I had experienced for only twenty days.

But I stood firmly by my decision. I wanted to try.

And eventually, they agreed.

Looking back, I think this moment captures my family’s teaching philosophy better than the word “explore” alone. They did not know whether my decision would work out. Neither did I. Their response to that uncertainty was not to pretend that the risks did not exist. They told me about them.

But they also did not treat uncertainty as a reason to stop me from trying.

At the time, my high school experience effectively followed a two-stage structure. During the first two years, we completed most of the required curriculum and the examinations needed for graduation. The final year was much more heavily oriented toward preparation for the national college entrance examination, or Gaokao, with much of the school day devoted to reviewing previously learned material and practicing for the exam.

My decision to study abroad meant stepping away from that path.

After completing my necessary training and passing the examinations required for my high school diploma, I left the regular school track. Instead of spending my final year preparing for the Gaokao with my classmates, I began preparing for a different route—studying English, taking standardized tests for U.S. college admission, and completing AP coursework.

For the first time, I was no longer following the educational path that had been laid out in front of me. I had chosen another one without knowing exactly where it would lead.

## Learning from Mistakes

Getting out of the traditional path was not easy.

School provides teachers, schedules, assignments, and examinations that push you to study even when you do not want to. The outside world would not  tell me when to study or how much progress I should make.

I had to learn how to navigate on my own.

I started by studying English at a private language school designed for students preparing to study abroad. At first, passion was enough to keep me working hard. Everything was new, and I had a clear destination in mind.

But moving from high-school English to the level required for the TOEFL and to studying and communicating comfortably in English was a long journey. Initial excitement could carry me for a few weeks, but not for four or five months. At some point, motivation had to be supplemented by self-control.

Phones and computers became one of the first challenges.

The irony was that I could not avoid them. The TOEFL was computer-based, so becoming comfortable reading, listening, writing, and responding on a computer was part of my preparation. For vocabulary, phone-based applications were far more efficient and useful to me. Technology was not something external to my learning environment. 

But the same devices also contained unlimited number of distractions. I could open my computer to practice for the TOEFL and end up watching a television show. I could pick up my phone to review vocabulary and find myself watching short videos instead. Computer games were another possibility, although I was never much of a gamer and rarely played them.

The problem was not as simple as whether phones and computers were good or bad for learning. 

After my first attempt at the TOEFL, I did not get a score I was satisfied with. I started to realize that I needed more self-control. But I never fully overcame this problem, as English was necessary for me, but learning English itself was not one of my interests.

Eventually, I passed the TOEFL with a score of 80, which meant that I could meet the minimum English-language requirement for admission to a U.S. college. Combined with a pretty decent SAT score, I was admitted to Binghamton University.
