Research Proposal Idea

Perception-Based Urban Routing

Understanding how perceptions of urban space shape the routes people choose — and the outcomes that follow.

Conventional routing models assume travelers respond to measurable attributes such as travel time, distance, cost, and network connectivity. Yet people also navigate cities through their perceptions of neighborhoods and places — avoiding certain areas, preferring familiar environments, or choosing routes that differ from those an algorithm calls optimal.

The Core Idea

Two cities, one origin and destination

For the same origin and destination, the algorithmic “optimal” route and the perception-influenced route can be very different. These differences may lead to different experiences of accessibility — and different patterns of opportunity in the city.

Where the Idea Comes From

Cities are navigated by mental maps, not only networks

Routing algorithms describe the city through objective, measurable information: time, distance, cost, and network connectivity. But in everyday life, people also rely on subjective judgments — Is this neighborhood safe? Is it pleasant? Is the detour worth it? Does this area feel connected to where I need to go?

When choosing between the same origin and destination, two people may take very different routes — not because the transportation network differs, but because their mental maps of the city differ. A neighborhood may feel unfamiliar, inconvenient, unsafe, or simply “not on the way,” even when an algorithm identifies it as part of an efficient route.

What happens when the city people perceive is different from the city represented by the transportation network?

What This Project Aims to Do

Three questions this project pursues

The broader goal is to understand how these behavioral differences may influence transportation outcomes, neighborhood connectivity, and patterns of urban economic activity.

01

Quantify perception in routing behavior

Quantify how perception shapes routing behavior across different neighborhood and city contexts.

02

Measure experienced accessibility

Examine how perception-driven routing creates systematic differences between modeled and experienced accessibility.

03

Trace broader urban outcomes

Explore how these differences influence broader urban and economic outcomes.

By connecting human perception with route choice and accessibility, this project seeks to inform more people-centered transportation planning and urban policy.
