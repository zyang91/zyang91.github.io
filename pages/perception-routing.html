Research Proposal Idea

Perception-Based Urban Routing

Two places can be the same distance from a destination and just as well served. People still use one and avoid the other.

People leave parts of the city out of the routes they will actually consider. Those exclusions are not random. They follow patterns across people and places, and they can pull experienced accessibility a long way from what a model predicts. I want to know when that happens, and whether planners should do anything about it.

The Core Idea

There is one modeled city, but multiple experienced cities

The metaphor is loose but useful. For one origin and destination, the modeled network offers an optimal route while the route a traveler will actually consider may be another. The same neighborhood can be familiar to longtime residents, threatening to outsiders, and simply avoided by older travelers.

The Modeled Network

Streets, transit, connections, and travel times as represented.

One Behaviorally Available Network

These exclusions are not noise. They can reflect real conditions and what people believe about those conditions at the same time. That puts the question between two bodies of work: how people read places, and how networks produce access.

  • Physical barriers
  • Safety and comfort
  • Familiarity and information
  • Reputation and historical narrative
  • Prior experience
  • Perception that lags changed conditions

The same street may belong to different practical choice sets at 2 p.m. and 11 p.m.

Why This Matters

Modeled accessibility is not experienced accessibility

Planning evaluates accessibility using the modeled network: where streets and transit exist, how long trips take, and which destinations can theoretically be reached. When parts of that network are systematically excluded from practical choice sets, a gap opens between modeled accessibility and experienced accessibility. It matters in three ways.

Effectiveness of Investment

Infrastructure can exist without delivering the accessibility a conventional model attributes to it. If people avoid a theoretically convenient street, station, or crossing, those measures overstate what the investment produces. Built does not necessarily mean behaviorally available.

Spatial Inequality

These gaps may not be evenly distributed across people or places. Differences in familiarity, past experience, perceived safety, physical ability, or knowledge of the network can leave two people facing the same modeled network with different sets of opportunities actually within reach.

Planning Diagnosis

The same observed mismatch may call for an environmental change, better information, a better model, or no intervention at all. Which one it is cannot be read off the size of the gap.

Can spatial inequality exist not only in the infrastructure people have access to, but also in the network they are willing to treat as available?

The Conceptual Backbone

Technically reachable is not the same as actually considered

The modeled network defines what is technically reachable. The behaviorally available network defines what people are actually willing to consider. The difference shapes experienced accessibility.

What narrows the second network is not idiosyncratic taste alone. Individual perception belongs here because it is systematically related to group position, prior experience, information, place, time, and mobility constraints.

Idiosyncratic perception is a curiosity. Socially patterned perception is a planning problem.

A Conceptual Framework

  1. 01

    Modeled Network

    Streets, transit, connections, and travel times as represented, and the modeled accessibility calculated from them.

  2. 02

    Behaviorally Available Network

    The routes and places a person keeps in their practical choice set. Social and spatial conditions narrow it.

  3. 03

    Experienced Accessibility

    Access as perceived, navigated, and realized in everyday life.

A possible feedback loop

  • Place reputation
  • Avoidance
  • Underuse
  • Disinvestment or changing conditions
  • back to reputation

The dashed links are hypotheses, not established effects. The loop is here to show one thing: an exclusion can outlast a single detour and start shaping the conditions that produced it. Reputation is one mechanism among four, not the whole project.

Physical connectivity does not necessarily imply experienced connectivity.

Project Questions

Three questions this project pursues

In the order the project would have to answer them.

01

Identify what gets excluded

Where the behaviorally available network departs from the modeled one, and whether those departures are patterned across people and places.

02

Measure experienced accessibility

Whether those exclusions add up to systematic differences between modeled and experienced accessibility.

03

Trace the effects on cities

A longer-term direction. Whether these differences aggregate into citywide urban and economic outcomes.

From Diagnosis to Intervention

Mismatch is a diagnosis, not a policy prescription

Knowing that perception narrows the network does not by itself tell planners what to do. A gap between the modeled network and the behaviorally available one can arise for very different reasons, and those mechanisms imply very different responses. So the first question is not how planners should change perception.

When does that gap become a planning problem, what produces it, and which intervention, if any, matches the mechanism?

  1. Step 01

    Identify the mismatch

    The modeled network on one side; the behaviorally available network on the other.

  2. Step 02

    Ask whether it produces a meaningful consequence

    Reduced access to opportunities, additional travel burden, underuse of transportation infrastructure, or unequal experienced accessibility.

    If not Intervention may not be warranted. Individual route preferences and differences in perception do not automatically constitute planning problems.

    If so Ask what mechanism produces the gap, because each one points toward a different kind of response.

  3. Step 03

    Match the response to the mechanism

    Four mechanisms can produce the same observed gap, and each implies a different intervention.

A

Physical or environmental barrier

Perception may reflect real characteristics of the environment: difficult crossings, poor pedestrian conditions, inadequate lighting, actual safety concerns, uncomfortable transit access, or physical discontinuities in the network.

Response Change the physical environment. The goal is not to persuade people that the barrier does not exist, but to address the condition they are responding to.

B

Information or familiarity barrier

A route may be physically usable, but people may lack information about it or feel unfamiliar with it. Here the infrastructure itself may not need to change.

Response Wayfinding, better travel information, navigation support, and opportunities to build familiarity with alternative routes.

C

Persistent or outdated perception

The physical environment may have changed while people's mental maps have not yet updated. A previously unpleasant or difficult route may keep being avoided long after improvements are made.

Response Information about improvements, exposure to changed environments, and experience-based interventions that let people update their mental maps.

How quickly do mental maps respond when the physical city changes?

D

Routing or modeling mismatch

The problem may lie partly in how transportation models represent travel cost.

What the model weighs time + distance + monetary cost + transfers

What travelers also weigh time + distance + monetary cost + transfers + conditions the model omits

Response Incorporate perception into routing and accessibility models, which could eventually support routing systems that represent how travelers actually experience urban networks rather than assuming the objectively shortest route is behaviorally equivalent for everyone.

An Open Research Agenda

  1. 01Detect the gap
  2. 02Measure its consequences
  3. 03Identify its mechanism
  4. 04Match the intervention
  5. 05Evaluate whether perception and behavior change

Intervention depends on diagnosis. This framing lets the project move beyond documenting differences in route perception, while avoiding the assumption that perception is something planners should automatically try to correct.

A perceived barrier may be wrong, outdated, or entirely reasonable. The existence of a gap does not tell planners which one it is.

The goal is not to make people use every route the model says they can use. It is to understand why some parts of the city fall outside people's practical choice sets, and whether planners should do anything about it.
